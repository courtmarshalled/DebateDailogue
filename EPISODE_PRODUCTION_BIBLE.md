# Episode Production Bible for AI Agents

> Original source: supplied `build_bible.py`; version 1, 5 November 2026. The following preserves the source text and its ten-section sequence.

## 01. Purpose and editorial authority

**Episode Production Bible for AI Agents**

*Jon’s investigative debate series  |  Version 1  |  5 November 2026*

Make difficult arguments understandable, present each side at its strongest, and help viewers judge the reasons. Real human conversations provide the entry point. AI extends the investigation through sourced arguments. Jon supplies the human curiosity, selection and judgment. Every creative or technical choice should serve that experience.

### The identity to preserve

Jon is a curious filmmaker who notices a real assumption, implication or unanswered question after others think the matter is settled. He is not a permanent streamer, an omniscient lecturer or a third debater. His opinion comes after a fair examination and includes reasons. Allow an argument to hold up. Do not manufacture doubt to sustain a character.

The broader ambition is to express what it means to learn with conversational AI while purpose and judgment remain human. Bring older thinkers into the conversation through their actual arguments and texts. Let this ambition emerge through the investigation rather than through a recurring speech about AI.

### Read authority correctly

- Current instructions from Jon govern the requested work. Read applicable AGENTS.md files and the current production prompts before editing their files. Follow the active schema and validator; resolve conflicts explicitly rather than quietly relaxing checks.

- Established preferences include fair steelmanning, concrete language, a curious host, honest sourcing and a final fact check. A saved recommendation or an earlier model’s confident assessment is not automatically Jon’s decision.

- Label suggestions as proposals. Physical desk openings, rankings, a new visual style and the eight unicorn openings remain candidates unless Jon has selected them. No audience improvement has been established by the research in this chat.

### Use the ten pages as a working reference

## 02. Team responsibilities and handoffs

Use clear ownership so several agents can improve an episode without competing edits or contradictory approvals. The roles below are a recommended allocation; one agent may perform several roles, except where the pipeline requires a fresh reader. Jon retains editorial judgment.

### Assign a named owner to each output

- Lead editor: records scope, chosen episode, active paths, selected packaging, substantive decisions and remaining issues. Assigns tasks and consolidates changes. Maintains one canonical production script.

- Researcher: verifies material claims against actual passages, records locations and limitations, and checks whether each source supports the wording. An evidence ledger is the output, not a list of plausible links.

- Writer: creates the argument plan and draft. Builds the strongest objection and reply for each side before compressing the dialogue. Supplies visual intentions separately from spoken lines.

- Editorial reviewer: checks steelmanning, attribution, argument continuity, host tilt and promise delivery. Records concrete defects and applies only authorized revisions. Approval identifies the exact current artifact.

- Blind readers: receive only the specified spoken sequence and reader prompt. No explanation of what the script intends, prior review, research, rankings or released-history context may coach their response.

- Production and QA: validates files, assembles media, inspects the visual proof and records actual listening. Technical validation, visual inspection and audio listening are separate checks.

### Make every handoff resumable

Include the task, input paths and versions, files the recipient owns, acceptance criteria, changed claims, unresolved issues and the next required action. Distinguish “proposed,” “applied,” “validated,” “editorially approved,” “rendered” and “publication checked.” Record actual command results; never infer a pass from file existence.

Keep one editor per shared file. Other agents propose changes or hand off ownership before editing it. Re-read the latest file before applying a patch. Preserve original media, source text, earlier reviews and useful backups. Do not overwrite a released episode merely to satisfy a new check.

### Control revision cost

Fix named problems after a clean pass; do not restart the entire script for cosmetic preferences. The current pipeline shares a four-round repair budget across exceptional repairs. Record diagnostics if it is exhausted. An edit renews the affected evidence and editorial review; settled final speech requires a new blind read.

> Basis: [R1, R5–R7]. Never expose credentials or copy .env contents. Team agreement is editorial evidence, not proof that an argument is true or that viewers will stay.

## 03. Plan the episode and its promise

Before writing dialogue, record the source conversation, requested scope, audience question and the particular thing that makes Jon curious. Use his supplied reaction when available. If it is missing, draft a question he could investigate; do not invent a belief, memory or emotional experience for him.

### Create an episode brief

- State the central question in everyday language. Identify each side’s burden, the relevant definitions and what the episode will not attempt to establish.

- Choose a complete source exchange with a claim, meaningful challenge and developing response. Confirm speaker identities and their stance in this conversation, including devil’s advocacy where supported.

- Write the crux: the disputed premise or inference the investigation repeatedly tests. Map every pause to a new contribution and identify what remains open afterward.

- Write the viewer promise: the question answered, distinction learned or capability gained by the ending. Name the exact section that delivers it. If there is no payoff, change the plan or the promise.

### Package the investigation

Jon rejected long, generic titles that merely named a topic. Prefer a short, understandable conflict or story with an answer the episode can deliver. “How Mormons Stole the West” was his example of a specific story promise, not a required syntactic template. How and Why are options, not mandatory openings.

“Do Unicorns Disprove God?” is the leading title explored in this chat, not a recorded publication selection. It is short and surprising, but its broad question creates a duty: distinguish challenging one proof from disproving God. A question mark does not excuse an implication the episode cannot address.

Let title, thumbnail and opening perform complementary jobs. The title poses the tension; the thumbnail makes the conflict quickly visible; the opening begins examining it. Avoid text-heavy duplication, invented reactions, false quotations and a thumbnail verdict the script cannot defend.

### Make rankings and scope honest

If ranking objections, define the criterion and retain its conditions. Ranking how well objections survive a defence does not rank religions or decide whether God exists. Four objections do not justify “every objection.” Present Jon’s placements and substantive judgments for his review rather than assigning him a verdict.

> Basis: [R3–R4, R6–R8] and the title discussion in this chat. Draft 3–5 schema-compatible title options; shortness is an editorial preference, while the current validator permits up to 70 characters.

## 04. Build the first minute

Begin the particular investigation before explaining the channel. Give an unfamiliar viewer enough context to understand the puzzle and a reason to continue. The first minute includes any source footage, host speech, physical action and delivery pauses; host word count alone is not its running time.

### Choose a mechanism suited to the material

- Demonstration: show a concrete thing, make a change, then expose a result that needs explaining. Veritasium’s helium opening develops two surprising observations before offering a broad lesson.

- Experiment: establish an ambitious test and its prediction, then challenge a familiar explanation. The two-clock opening makes a difficult question measurable.

- Story or working object: follow an intriguing action or use an object immediately, then uncover its wider significance or governing rule. The fiber and Enigma openings use these approaches.

- Stakes and reframing: show an extraordinary development, explain its consequence, then reveal a less obvious meaning. Cleo’s Neuralink opening follows this progression.

- Journey or precise question: put viewers inside an unfolding experience, or narrow a large curiosity into an answerable investigation with a credible route to the answer. The Sun and aliens openings offer different examples.

### Treat the creator study as limited evidence

The 5 November study used English captions from the five latest full-length uploads on each channel’s Videos tab. Shorts were excluded; Veritasium’s channel announcement was retained and identified as atypical. Footage retrieval failed. These are interpretations of first-minute writing, not verified shot analysis, retention data or universal creator formulas. [R9]

The study does not establish that every intro needs a prop, a spoken title question in sentence one, a formal “by the end” promise or exactly 60 seconds. The ten-episode desk-shot preview was a proposal. Preserve discovery: do not summarize the explanation the source is about to deliver.

### Apply it to unicorn

Candidate openings include trying the argument on a unicorn, testing the move from an idea to reality, or following the objection after it seems decisive. Keep the proof understandable before evaluating it. Identify Alex, Soul Boom and the AI roles briefly when needed. The eight attempts are alternatives, not one composite script.

Build a timed rough cut. Check title alignment, spoken comprehension, what changes during the opening and the first meaningful payoff. Avoid routine “watch for” or “now listen” filler: the current production prompts reject it despite its appearance in some preview drafts. A genuine instruction needs an approved wording compatible with the active contract.

> Basis: [R3–R4, R6, R9]. A proposed desk shot or rearranged cold open needs a verified editing route before it becomes a production commitment.

## 05. Research and source integrity

AI output, a plausible citation and agreement among agents cannot establish an external claim. Check the actual source passage and whether it supports the precise wording, scope and attribution. Jon specifically wants a final fact-check pass, with disputed interpretations and uncertainty made clear to viewers.

### Keep four kinds of content distinct

- Source evidence: what an inspected text, recording or dataset actually says. Preserve its context, date, relevant population and qualifications.

- Interpretation: what an author or speaker could mean. Attribute the reading and acknowledge a credible alternative where it matters.

- Agent reconstruction: a stronger defence, modern example or application composed for this episode. Never present it as the historical thinker’s words or the human speaker’s intended meaning.

- Creative proposal: a visual, hook, ranking or retention hypothesis. Label its status rather than giving it the authority of evidence or Jon’s approval.

### Maintain a claim ledger

For each material claim, record a stable ID, exact authored wording, current location, claim type, source and locator, inspected passage, verification result and limitation. Material claims include quotations, named scholarly attributions, numerical results, studies, contested assertions and consequential broad inferences. Local reasoning may be explained directly without an ornamental citation.

Prefer primary texts and original research; use scholarly treatments to clarify disputes. Search snippets, bibliographies and summaries are leads, not evidence that a book was read. Check dates, units, denominators, comparison groups and whether a paraphrase changes the conclusion. Use the current research-context and research validation tools for production artifacts.

### Handle uncertainty visibly

If retrieval fails, report the failure. Remove or replace the claim, or retain it only with an explicit limitation and the required recorded disposition. The current pipeline requires an audible qualification at the affected location for a qualified material claim. A tiny footnote alone does not correct an unqualified spoken assertion. Footnotes may explain uncertainty or alternative readings, but must be accurate and readable.

### Protect the source conversation

Use creator-supplied original media for episode production. Check unlabeled speakers by ear before attribution. Copy cut anchors from the generated timestamped transcript exactly. A supplied transcript can clarify names and speakers but does not replace the production clock. Do not invent expressions, gestures, reactions, quotations, timestamps or motives.

> Basis: [R1, R3, R5–R7]. For unicorn, keep the modern unicorn application distinct from Anselm’s original text and distinguish this version from later ontological arguments.

## 06. Write the strongest opposing arguments

Red and Blue argue positions, not impersonations of the people in the clip. Their job is to add a deeper, better-sourced examination than the original conversation supplies. Do not assign permanent religious or political identities to colors; use the current episode’s side definitions consistently.

### Plan each exchange before writing it

- State the actual opponent claim and its strongest relevant version. Identify the premise, inference or evidence being challenged.

- Choose the strongest objection and the strongest available reply. Include a fair concession only where the reasoning warrants it. State what survives the concession.

- Identify the new contribution and open issue. Later exchanges should develop that issue rather than replay the same contrast in new words.

Every line should answer the previous move or advance the argument. Label its intellectual action honestly: define, distinguish, formalize, hidden_premise, steelman, objection, reply, counterexample, evidence, concession or implication. A label does not turn praise, recap or a preview into an argument.

### Make depth understandable

Write for a smart 13-year-old hearing the episode. Explain the concrete example before naming the technical term. Use agents and actions: “Blue asks how imagining something shows it exists” is clearer than “Blue contests the inference from conception to existence.” Keep contractions, connected sentences and ordinary words. Avoid a chain of punchy fragments, unnecessary test names, em dashes and stage directions in speech.

Use one clear picture per idea and reuse it where it helps. Preserve conditions when shortening. Do not turn “if these assumptions hold” into an unconditional result. Do not introduce a new thinker, argument or framework in the final round. The humans should raise the clip’s striking example before an AI response develops it.

### Audit fairness rather than assuming it

For each side, list the two or three central arguments and replies a thoughtful defender expects. Mark each present, thin or missing; restore any central omission. Apply the proponent test: would a defender recognize this as their best case? Check word counts, concessions, last words and host tilt. Mechanical balance is a diagnostic, not a reason to invent equal concessions.

A length edit must not quietly remove one side’s strongest defence. A reply does not automatically defeat an objection. A failed proof does not establish the opposite conclusion. Neither AI declares a winner, and Jon’s synthesis must retain the conditions the exchange established.

> Basis: [R3, R6–R7]. Current planning requires at least three plain-language advanced insights in shelf_cookie entries; explain them rather than using scholarly names as decoration.

## 07. Write Jon and deliver the ending

Jon earns his place through the detail he notices and the question he pursues. He can be warm, quietly funny and personally involved without becoming a commentator who narrates every turn. Use his actual observations. Do not invent private beliefs, experiences, expertise or a dramatic change of mind.

### Give each interruption a specific job

Pause after a complete argument or meaningful thought. The stop points to the relevant words just heard and asks a question that the AI exchange can answer. Name the AI that speaks first when the active schema requires it. Avoid explaining the answer before the debaters work through it.

A useful unicorn stop asks what being real adds, or whether defining a greatest being establishes that anything matches the definition. It should make the reasoning easier to follow without treating the objection or defence as settled.

### Give wayfinding without repeating the debate

A takeaway says what the exchange established, which condition remains open or why the next part matters. Keep it brief and faithful. Delete a takeaway that simply rephrases all the AI lines. Do not give the host a fresh argument that bypasses the opposing reply.

When Jon offers a judgment, include the reason and what would change it. Avoid repeated declarations of fairness or curiosity. Show those qualities by testing a convincing objection, allowing a reply time and acknowledging when evidence alters the investigation.

### Pay off the actual title

Return to the opening object or question when it clarifies the result. Answer at the level the material supports. State the strongest reply, what it achieved, what it did not establish and the unresolved premise. One accessible question can invite comments; do not end by launching a new debate.

For “Do Unicorns Disprove God?”, distinguish three questions: does the unicorn copy the argument faithfully; does the strongest defence defeat that objection; and does the argument establish that God exists? These questions can have different answers. Even defeating this proof would not disprove God.

### Use history and human stakes where they work

A historical anecdote belongs at the moment its idea changes the reasoning. Verify it before recording. Do not move every story into the intro or force one into every episode. A personal consequence or reflective ending should follow the evidence and Jon’s real perspective, rather than add emotion to compensate for an unclear conclusion.

> Basis: [R2–R4, R6–R8]. Rankings and Jon’s substantive verdicts remain proposals until he approves them. Do not script a discovery such as “this changed everything for me” without his confirmation.

## 08. Plan the picture and sound together

For each important beat, specify the spoken line, what viewers see, what they should notice, the reveal trigger and any sound intention. Keep those instructions outside spoken JSON fields. The essential argument should remain understandable by listening; the image adds precision and discovery.

### Give every object a reasoning job

A unicorn, apple, die, cake or argument sheet should show a comparison, assumption or change. An object is not useful merely because hands touch it. A prop illustrates the test; it does not prove the philosophical conclusion. Reuse an established object at later pauses and the ending when the relationship is meaningful.

Start physical action mid-process when that is the chosen opening. Avoid obligatory walk-ins, greetings, workshop symbolism and staged tool handling. A physical opening is one possible approach, not an established requirement for all episodes. Do not reveal a source example early if the selected structure depends on the humans introducing it.

### Make evidence legible

Board notes should be things a student could write down: a claim, distinction, condition, burden, concession or unresolved question. They are not duplicate subtitles or decorative slogans. Cue them with literal phrases from the host’s current speech. They must not add a verdict or claim the spoken reasoning never supports.

Show source text accurately and mark paraphrases. Preserve quotations rather than rewriting them to fit. Footnotes must remain readable during their display time. Check full-size frames and a phone-size preview; avoid competing captions, cramped labels and speaker tags covering content.

### Use the active visual system

Read the current cut-in style guide and episode configuration before drawing. As inspected, original drawing layouts are the default; editorial and Vox variations are selectable alternatives. Do not substitute a redesign because an old preview looks attractive. Preserve word-triggered reveals, coordinate scaling, safe areas and stable speaker identity. Inspect motion as well as final arrangements.

### Separate atmosphere from evidence

Music, lighting, dramatic graphics and confident voices can guide attention; they cannot establish truth or imply an unsupported winner. Identify illustrative or synthetic imagery where confusion would matter. Watch the complete assembly for awkward pauses, cut sentences and mismatched demonstrations. Listen for pronunciation, cadence, source-to-host levels and transitions.

> Basis: [R3–R4, R5–R7, R10]. No transcript-only review may claim to have checked delivery, gestures or sound. A contact sheet checks sampled composition; it cannot establish motion or complete audio quality.

## 09. Honor the production contract

Read the live code/AGENTS.md, README.md and stage prompts at the start of production. This page is a dated reference, not a replacement for executable rules. The current pipeline takes a creator-supplied full original MP4 in its original language and best available quality. A URL supplies attribution and metadata. It does not authorize downloading production footage.

### Keep editorial and production artifacts separate

The author writes argument_plan.json and reaction_draft.json. Research writes research.json. A blind draft read produces comprehension.json. The editor owns reaction.json and writes editorial_review.json last after snap, validation and editorial-context. Generate spoken_final.txt in playback order, save audience-context before the fresh final read, then bind final_comprehension.json. Full rendering requires current evidence, approval and final reading before TTS.

Run pipeline.py stages with the repository’s .venv/bin/python and --episode pointing to the selected folder. From an existing agent session, follow the stages directly; do not recursively launch make_video.py to start another agent workflow. Use preview for inexpensive iteration.

### Current core limits

- Source span 150–480 seconds; 4–7 pauses at least 10 seconds apart; 3–5 alternating AI lines per pause; each AI line 8–52 words and exactly one valid move.

- Intro 2–4 parts of 8–70 words, with 2–4 teaching notes per part. Optional host stops 8–110 words; stops are supplied for every pause or none. Takeaways at most 200 characters. Wrap 25–150 words.

- Crux 8–30 words, developed at 2–4 crux pauses. Footnotes at most 130 characters. Grade ceiling 10 per checked passage; AI-line average ceiling 8. Packaging has 3–5 titles, each at most 70 characters.

These values were inspected in reaction/script.py on 5 November. Runtime estimates are not measured delivery; README guidance and executable ranges differ, so check the live report. Never pad a script to meet a suggested running time.

### Protect cuts and supported fields

Copy just_said, resume_with and span.start_with verbatim from the generated transcript. Snap against that clock and inspect the actual cut. Keep source.video_id and URL consistent. Include the required host point fields and exact cue phrases. Do not add worksheet, score, crowd or ai_cue fields. Store storyboards separately unless an existing supported field serves them.

The November preview flags filmed desk openings, a cold open between host sections and delayed AI appearance as unimplemented in its target pipeline. Recheck current capabilities. Use an explicitly planned hand edit or tested implementation; valid word counts do not make those sequences renderable. Leave live episodes untouched when preparing proposals.

> Basis: [R5–R7] and the supplied ten-episode preview. Final outputs are final.mp4, youtube.md and reaction.json; split layouts may place media under renders and work files under cache.

## 10. Release checks and source map

“Ready” must identify the stage. A script can be ready to record while the episode is not ready to publish. Jon reviews the complete spoken sequence, approves or requests changes, films the needed parts, and watches the complete finished video before uploading. Build a concrete reviewable result before requesting a decision.

### Confirm these before delivery

- Editorial: title promise answered; both sides pass the proponent test; reasoning and conditions survive the ending; material claims supported, qualified, removed or replaced; final fact check complete.

- Artifact integrity: current validation, research, editorial and blind-reader artifacts match the script. Generate new approvals for changed content; never refresh old hashes to simulate review. Render provenance matches the finished media.

- Playback: verify streams and duration; inspect the manifest and contact sheet under the visual-check prompt; review the actual motion and audio. Record specific observations and timestamps. Required pilot listening must be human or audio-capable.

- Delivery: provide the finished video, posting package and canonical script, identify remaining qualified claims and substantive judgments, and confirm the selected output path. Publication and paid services remain within the current authorization.

Known preview issues to recheck include the free-will video ID and URL mismatch, unverified speaker attributions and historical claims, the design episode’s incomplete revision, and opening sequences requiring new editing support. These were reported issues, not fresh checks of their current state.
